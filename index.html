Clubs

Setup and technique for every club in the bag. Tap a club to expand it. These are general starting points — every golfer's build and swing is a little different, so use them as a baseline to build from.

Driver

Typical distance: 200–280+ yards depending on player, but your own average matters more than any chart.

Ball position: Forward, off the inside of your lead heel.

Stance: Widest stance in the bag — shoulder-width or a touch wider, for stability at high speed.

Tee height: Roughly half the ball should sit above the top of the driver's crown.

Setup: Tilt your spine slightly away from the target, with a touch more weight on your trail side.

Swing thought: Sweep the ball off the tee on a shallow, ascending path — try to hit "up" on it, not down.

Common mistake: Ball too far back in the stance, causing a steep, descending strike (pop-ups, slices). Move it forward and tee it higher.

Fairway Woods

Typical distance: 180–230 yards.

Ball position: Just forward of center, roughly 2–3 ball-widths inside your lead heel.

Stance: Slightly narrower than driver.

Swing thought: Sweep the turf rather than dig — "brush the grass" instead of taking a divot.

Common mistake: Trying to scoop the ball into the air. Trust the loft, hit down and through, and let the club do the work.

Hybrids

Typical distance: 170–200 yards — a long-iron replacement.

Ball position: Center to slightly forward of center.

Swing thought: A shallower version of an iron strike — slightly descending, but more sweeping than a long iron.

Best for: Tight lies, rough, or long approaches where a long iron feels hard to control.

Long Irons (3–5)

Typical distance: 150–190 yards.

Ball position: Just forward of center.

Setup: Athletic posture, weight balanced roughly 50/50.

Swing thought: Compress the ball with a descending blow — ball first, then turf.

Common mistake: Trying to lift the ball with your hands. Trust the club's loft, hit down, and let the ball fly up on its own.

Mid Irons (6–8)

Typical distance: 120–160 yards.

Ball position: Center of stance.

Setup: Slightly narrower than long irons, with a bit more weight forward (around 55% lead side).

Swing thought: Descending strike — ball, then a shallow divot just after it.

These tend to be the most forgiving clubs in the bag — great ones to build consistent ball-striking on.

Short Irons & Wedges (9-iron–LW)

Typical distance: 50–130 yards, depending on club and swing length.

Ball position: Center to slightly back of center.

Setup: Narrower stance, hands slightly ahead of the ball at address.

Swing thought: A steeper, more controlled swing — accelerate through impact rather than decelerating into it.

Common mistake: Trying to scoop the ball up. Let your hands lead the clubhead through impact and trust the loft.

Chipping & Pitching

Ball position: Back of center for chips, center for pitches.

Setup: Narrow stance, weight favouring your lead side (roughly 60–70%), hands ahead of the ball.

Technique: A quiet, putting-like motion for chips with minimal wrist hinge; a bit more arm and wrist action for longer pitches.

Key principle: Get the ball rolling as soon as it's safely on the green — pick the club that lands it just on and lets it release to the hole, rather than always reaching for the same wedge.

Short game is worth disproportionate practice time — see the Strategy tab.

Putter

Ball position: Just forward of center, under your lead eye (drop a ball from your eye and check it lands on/near the ball).

Setup: Eyes over or just inside the ball, arms hanging naturally.

Stroke: A pendulum motion driven by the shoulders, with a quiet lower body and consistent tempo — think equal length back and through for a given distance.

Common mistake: Wrists breaking down through the stroke, causing inconsistent contact. Keep the wrists quiet and let the shoulders and arms do the work.

Swing Fundamentals

The building blocks of a repeatable swing, in the order they happen. Tap a section to expand it.

Grip

Types: Overlap (Vardon), interlock, or ten-finger (baseball) — overlap is the most common.

Neutral grip: Looking down, you should see roughly 2–3 knuckles of your lead hand, with the "V" shapes formed by both thumbs and forefingers pointing toward your trail shoulder.

Pressure: Light — like holding a tube of toothpaste without squeezing any out, around a 4–5 out of 10. Tension kills clubhead speed.

Setup & Posture

Feet: Shoulder-width for irons, a touch wider for driver.

Knees: Slight flex — an athletic, ready position.

Spine: Bend from the hips, not the waist, with a relatively straight back and your chin up off your chest.

Arms: Hang naturally from the shoulders — don't reach for the ball or crowd it.

Weight: Balanced on the balls of your feet, roughly 50/50 for irons.

Alignment

Aim the clubface at the target first, then build your stance around it.

Feet, hips, and shoulders should sit parallel to the target line, like railroad tracks — your body line runs parallel left of the target (for a right-handed golfer), not directly at it.

Check this often with alignment sticks or a spare club on the range — most amateurs aim further right than they realise.

Backswing

Takeaway: Move the club back low and slow, controlled by the shoulders and arms together, not just the hands.

Wrist hinge: Let your wrists hinge naturally as the club reaches waist height.

Shoulder turn: Rotate your shoulders around your spine (aim for roughly 80–90°) while keeping your lower body relatively stable.

Weight shift: Let your weight move into your trail foot as you turn back.

Downswing & Transition

Start the downswing with your lower body — hips beginning to unwind before the arms and club — rather than throwing the club from the top with your hands. This sequencing is one of the biggest differences between good and poor ball-strikers.

Avoid "casting" (releasing the wrist hinge too early from the top). Try to hold that angle a little longer than feels natural — often described as "lag."

Let the arms and club follow the body's rotation rather than swinging independently of it.

Impact

Hands slightly ahead of the clubhead at impact, especially with irons.

Weight has shifted onto the lead foot — roughly 80–90% for irons.

Hips are open toward the target, with the chest following just behind.

Follow-Through & Finish

Rotate fully through to a balanced finish, weight completely on the lead foot, belt buckle facing the target.

Hold your finish for a second or two after every shot on the range — it's a simple, effective way to check your balance and whether the swing was truly completed.

Tempo & Rhythm

A smooth, unhurried tempo — often described as roughly a 3:1 backswing-to-downswing ratio — produces more consistent contact than swinging as hard as possible.

Try counting in your head ("one-two" back, "three" down) on the range to build a repeatable rhythm.

Common Swing Faults & Fixes

Slice (curves hard right, for a right-hander)

Usually caused by an open clubface at impact and/or an out-to-in swing path.

Fix: Check your grip isn't too weak, and work on swinging more from inside the target line.

Hook (curves hard left, for a right-hander)

Usually caused by a closed clubface at impact and/or an in-to-out swing path.

Fix: Check your grip isn't too strong, and soften your release through impact.

Topping or thin shots

Usually caused by standing up out of your posture during the swing, catching the equator of the ball.

Fix: Keep your spine angle steady from address through impact — "stay in your posture."

Fat shots (hitting behind the ball)

Usually caused by weight hanging back on the trail foot at impact, or the ball positioned too far forward.

Fix: Make sure your weight actively shifts onto the lead side through impact.

Shanks (hitting off the hosel)

Usually caused by weight shifting too far toward your toes, or the club moving too far outward in transition.

Fix: Focus on staying centered and balanced, and feel like you're swinging from the heel of the club.

Casting (losing wrist lag)

Releasing the wrist angle too early from the top of the backswing, before the club reaches the ball.

Fix: Feel like the butt end of the grip leads the clubhead down into the ball, rather than the clubhead being thrown at it.

Course Strategy & Practice

How to actually lower your scores — course management, practice habits, and the mental side. Tap a section to expand it.

Course Management

Play to the widest part of the fairway and the fat part of the green, not always straight at the flag.

Take whatever club gets you safely past a hazard — a mid-iron in play beats a hero 3-wood into the trees.

Think "next shot": pick the spot that leaves you the easiest possible following shot, not just the longest drive.

Club Selection

Take one more club than you think on approach shots — most amateurs come up short far more often than they go long.

Know your realistic average distance with each club, not your best-ever shot, and club accordingly.

When in doubt, take the extra club and make a smooth swing rather than forcing a shorter one.

Pre-Shot Routine

Pick a precise target before every shot — not just "somewhere down there."

Take one or two practice swings feeling the shot you want to hit, then commit. Avoid over-thinking once you're stood over the ball.

Use the same routine every time — it builds consistency and helps under pressure.

Managing Your Misses

Everyone misses shots. The goal is making your misses as harmless as possible, rather than chasing perfect ones.

If you're fighting a slice mid-round, play for it rather than trying to fix it on the course — save the technical work for the range.

A comfortable bogey is a fine outcome. Trying to be a hero from trouble is where big scores happen.

Short Game Priority

Roughly 60% of your shots in a typical round happen from 100 yards and in, so it's worth weighting your practice time there over the driving range.

A solid short game — chipping and putting — lowers your scores faster than raw swing speed does.

Mental Game

Play one shot at a time — don't let a bad hole carry into the next one.

Stay patient. Golf is a game of misses, even for professionals.

Focus on process — routine, target, commitment — rather than outcome while you're actually swinging.

Reading Conditions

Wind: Into the wind, take more club and swing easier; downwind, take less club. Crosswinds affect a curving shot more than a straight one.

Slopes: An uphill lie tends to send the ball higher and shorter (take more club); a downhill lie sends it lower and longer (take less).

Rough: Expect less spin and a "flyer" that goes further with less control — take more club and aim for the center of the green.

Warm-Up Routine

Start with stretches and short, easy swings before hitting full shots.

Work from wedges up to driver, then finish with a few putts to get a feel for green speed before you tee off.

Hit a few shots with the club you'll use off the first tee last, so it's fresh in your hands.

Practice Habits

Split practice time roughly 50/50 between full swing and short game/putting.

Practice with a target and a purpose — alternate clubs, vary targets — rather than hitting the same shot on autopilot.

Track your rounds (which is exactly what the Handicap Calculator side of this app is for) to see real trends in your scoring, not just how it felt.
